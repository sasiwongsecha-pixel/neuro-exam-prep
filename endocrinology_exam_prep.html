📏 Short Stature

Height SDS < −2 for age and sex — Systematic Approach
DefinitionApproachCausesBone Age
⏱️ Onset & Pattern to Identify
  • From birth (SGA): IUGR, Russell-Silver syndrome, chromosomal
  • Early childhood (<5y): Syndromic causes, skeletal dysplasia, GH deficiency
  • School age: CDGP, familial short stature, chronic disease, hypothyroidism
  • Pubertal: Hypogonadism (Turner, Klinefelter), precocious puberty (accelerated then early fusion)
  • Always note: growth velocity, crossing percentiles (↓ velocity = pathologic)
🔎 Clinical Approach — Step by Step
1
Define short stature

Height < −2 SD for age/sex OR < −2 SDS from mid-parental height. Plot on growth chart!

2
Assess proportions: Proportionate vs Disproportionate

Measure arm span, U:L segment ratio. Disproportionate → skeletal dysplasia or hypothyroidism

3
Look for dysmorphic features

Turner (webbed neck, cubitus valgus), Noonan, Down, Prader-Willi, Russell-Silver

4
Bone age (X-ray left hand/wrist)

Delayed BA: CDGP, hypothyroid, GH deficiency, malnutrition. Advanced BA: Precocious puberty, CAH

5
Calculate mid-parental height

Male: (Father + Mother + 13)/2. Female: (Father + Mother − 13)/2. ±8.5 cm target range.

6
Endocrine workup if pathologic suspected

TSH/FT4, GH stimulation tests, IGF-1, sex hormones (FSH/LH), karyotype (girls), cortisol

⚠️ Key Clinical Clues — Normal Variants
  • CDGP: Delayed bone age (BA < CA by ≥2y), family history of late puberty, normal growth velocity, spontaneous pubertal development
  • Familial short stature: Normal BA = CA, short parents, normal growth velocity
  • Both (combined CDGP + FSS): Most common combination in clinic
🔬 Physical Findings by Cause
  • Turner: Webbed neck, cubitus valgus, shield chest, low hairline, streak gonads
  • Hypothyroid: U:L ratio ↑ (immature), delayed BA, cold intolerance, constipation
  • Cushing: Central obesity, striae, buffalo hump, moon face, HTN
  • GH def: Frontal bossing, doll-like face, micropenis (male), infantile fat distribution
  • Achondroplasia: Rhizomelia, macrocephaly, trident hand, midface hypoplasia
  • AHO (PHP): Round face, obesity, brachydactyly, Archibald sign

🃏 Key Fact Flashcards
What defines short stature?
Height < −2 SD below mean for age and sex (below 3rd percentile) OR < −2 SDS from mid-parental height
CDGP bone age finding?
Bone Age < Chronological Age by ≥2 years. BA parallels height age, not chronological age
Short stature + obesity clues
Cushing syndrome, Prader-Willi, Pseudohypoparathyroidism (AHO), Hypothyroidism, GH deficiency
Disproportionate short stature causes
Skeletal dysplasia (achondroplasia), Hypothyroidism, Rickets/metabolic bone disease, McCune-Albright

🔬 Turner Syndrome

45,X karyotype — Most common sex chromosome abnormality in females
Prevalence 1:250045,X or mosaicGonadal failure
⏱️ Onset & Presentation Timing
  • Fetal/Neonatal: Hydrops fetalis, cystic hygroma, lymphedema of hands/feet, left-sided CHD at birth
  • Childhood: Short stature (usually noticed by 5–6 years), recurrent otitis media, learning difficulties
  • Adolescence: Failure to enter puberty (primary amenorrhea), absent breast development → often the presenting complaint
  • Adulthood: Infertility, osteoporosis, cardiovascular complications (coarctation, bicuspid AoV, aortic dissection)
🔎 Clinical Approach
1
Suspect Turner in any girl with unexplained short stature

Even without classic dysmorphic features (mosaic may be subtle)

2
Karyotype (gold standard)

Peripheral blood. FISH if mosaicism suspected. Look for Y chromosome material → gonadoblastoma risk

3
Cardiac evaluation

Echo for bicuspid aortic valve, coarctation, aortic dimensions. MRI for aorta in adults

4
Endocrine workup

FSH/LH (elevated = gonadal failure), AMH (ovarian reserve), TSH (autoimmune thyroid), glucose

5
Treatment: GH + Estrogen replacement

GH from ~9y to optimize height. Estrogen from ~11–12y for pubertal induction. Add progestin once breakthrough bleeding or after 2 years

⚠️ Classic Clinical Clues
  • Short stature (80%) — most common presenting sign
  • Webbed neck (pterygium colli) — 50% — POSTERIOR
  • Cubitus valgus (68%) — carrying angle increased
  • Shield chest — widely spaced nipples
  • Lymphedema hands and feet (especially neonatal)
  • Low posterior hairline
  • Nail dysplasia, high-arched palate
  • Multiple pigmented nevi (50%)
  • Archibald sign (+) — 4th metacarpal short
🔬 Cardiac & Lab Findings
  • Heart (LEFT-SIDED): Bicuspid AoV, coarctation of aorta, elongated transverse arch, PAPVR, ASD/VSD
  • FSH/LH: Elevated (hypergonadotropic hypogonadism)
  • AMH <0.56 ng/mL: Predicted no ovarian function
  • Inhibin B: Undetectable
  • 45,X: Highest morbidity, highest bicuspid AoV
  • Mosaic (45X/46XX): Most favorable phenotype, possible fertility
  • 45X/46XY: Gonadoblastoma risk 4–60% → gonadectomy

🧬 Noonan Syndrome

RAS/MAPK pathway mutation — Phenotypically similar to Turner but different genetics
Prevalence 1:2500PTPN11 50%AD
⏱️ Onset
  • Birth: Characteristic facies, CHD (pulmonary stenosis most common), reduced birth weight possible
  • Childhood: Short stature, developmental delay, learning difficulties, cryptorchidism (males)
  • Adolescence: Delayed puberty, bleeding disorder (factor deficiency), hypogonadism in males
⚠️ Key Clinical Clues
  • Triangular facies — classic
  • Hypertelorism (100%) — widely-spaced eyes
  • Pectus excavatum or carinatum (50%)
  • Scoliosis common
  • Short neck (100%)
  • LOW-SET ears, ptosis
  • Cryptorchidism (75%) in males → infertility
  • Myeloproliferative disorders (juvenile CMML)
  • Bleeding tendency (factor deficiencies)
  • Right-sided CHD: PS (most common), HCM, ASD, VSD
  • Mild cognitive impairment (25%)
🧬 Genetics
  • PTPN11 (50%) → RAS/MAPK activation
  • SOS1 (10%), RAF1 (10%), KRAS (2%), BRAF (2%)
  • Autosomal dominant, new mutation in 50%
  • 46,XY or 46,XX — normal karyotype
💊 Endocrine Associations
  • Impaired fertility in males (cryptorchidism, Sertoli dysfunction)
  • Preserved fertility in females
  • Decreased bone mineral density
  • GH deficiency possible → GH therapy indicated
  • Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism less common

💉 Growth Hormone Deficiency

Insufficient GH → impaired linear growth + metabolic effects
Idiopathic (most common)CongenitalAcquired
⏱️ Onset & Age of Presentation
  • Neonatal: Hypoglycemia (esp. male with micropenis), prolonged jaundice → think SOD, pituitary aplasia
  • Early childhood: Deceleration of height velocity, "doll-like" appearance, frontal bossing
  • School age: Progressive short stature, delayed puberty despite normal gonadotropins, sparse hair
  • Acquired (any age): CNS tumor (craniopharyngioma #1), radiation, trauma — height velocity drop is key clue
  • Mean age in Thai clinic: 8.0 years at diagnosis
🔎 Diagnostic Approach
1
Screen: IGF-1 level (age-specific)

Low IGF-1 is highly suggestive but not diagnostic alone. False low: liver disease, poor nutrition, hypothyroidism

2
Confirmation: 2 GH stimulation tests

Insulin Tolerance Test (gold standard) OR arginine, clonidine, glucagon. Peak GH <7–10 µg/L = deficient

3
MRI pituitary

Look for structural defects, SOD, absent posterior bright spot, small anterior pituitary, tumors

4
Assess other pituitary hormones

Multiple pituitary hormone deficiency common with structural lesions. Check TSH, cortisol, LH/FSH

🔬 Physical Findings — Classic "Doll-Like" Face
  • Frontal bossing
  • Underdeveloped nasal bridge
  • Retarded facial bone growth
  • Micropenis (male neonatal)
  • Infantile fat distribution ("doll-like")
  • Decreased musculature
  • High pitched voice
  • Sparse, thin hair + slow nail growth
  • Delayed fontanel closure
  • Relatively normal skeletal proportions (vs hypothyroid)
📋 Causes of GH Deficiency
Idiopathic ▶
Most common overall. Example: Lionel Messi was treated with GH. No structural lesion found.
Congenital / Structural ▶
Septo-optic dysplasia (SOD), agenesis of corpus callosum, holoprosencephaly
Midline defects: single central incisor, cleft lip/palate
Acquired ▶
CNS tumors: Craniopharyngioma #1, germinoma, lymphoma
Cranial radiation (dose-dependent)
Perinatal/postnatal trauma, CNS infection
Thalassemia + Hemochromatosis ▶
Iron deposition → pituitary damage
GH def, hypogonadism, hypothyroidism (secondary), DM, adrenal insufficiency
MRI: hypointensity on T2-WI, low T1 signal, reduced pituitary volume
⚠️ GH Treatment Complications to Know
  • Intracranial hypertension
  • Worsening scoliosis
  • Slipped capital femoral epiphysis
  • Worsening obstructive sleep apnea
  • Pancreatitis
  • Increased insulin resistance

🦴 Metabolic Bone Diseases — Overview

Osteoporosis vs Osteomalacia vs PHPT — The Big Three Comparison
OsteoporosisOsteomalaciaPHPT
🔎 Clinical Approach to Metabolic Bone Disease
1
History: fracture, bone pain, deformity, dental problems

Low trauma fracture = pathological. Note: skull, face, fingers, toes NOT counted in osteoporosis

2
Look for extraskeletal clues

Blue sclera (OI, Marfan), band keratopathy (hypoparathyroidism), hearing loss (Paget, OI), skin lesions (MEN1, McCune-Albright)

3
Labs: Ca, PO4, ALP, PTH, 25(OH)D

This triad distinguishes the three major diseases. See table below.

4
Imaging: X-ray, DXA scan, bone scan

Looser zones = osteomalacia (pathognomonic). Rugger-jersey spine = PHPT. Vertebral fractures.

⚡ Rickets / Osteomalacia

Impaired bone mineralization — Calcipenic vs Phosphopenic types
Vitamin D defX-linked HPRTIOAlways ↑ ALP
⏱️ Onset & Clinical Presentation
  • Rickets (children, open epiphyses): Gradually worsening bony deformities from standing/walking age. Onset depends on severity of deficiency
  • Vitamin D deficiency rickets: Often 6–24 months, especially breastfed infants without supplementation
  • X-linked hypophosphatemic rickets (XLH): Appears when child starts weight-bearing (~1 year), X-linked dominant, males more severe
  • Tumor-induced osteomalacia (TIO): Adults, insidious onset over months-years, muscle weakness + bone pain + fragility fractures
  • Osteomalacia (adults): Often asymptomatic early; bone pain, proximal muscle weakness, fatigue
🔎 Approach: Calcipenic vs Phosphopenic
Calcipenic Rickets:
  • Low Ca (except mild-moderate Vit D def)
  • Low/normal P
  • ↑↑ PTH (secondary hyperPTH)
  • Causes: Vit D deficiency, VDDR Type 1 & 2, Ca deficiency, liver/renal disease
Phosphopenic Rickets:
  • Normal Ca
  • Low P (always!)
  • Normal or slightly elevated PTH
  • Causes: XLH (FGF23↑), TIO (FGF23↑), Fanconi, tenofovir
✅ Always: ALP is elevated in both types (400–800 IU/L in hypophosphatemic rickets)
🔬 Physical Findings — Rickets in Children
  • Rachitic rosary — beaded swelling at costochondral junctions
  • Harrison's groove — horizontal groove below chest
  • Craniotabes — soft skull bones
  • Frontal bossing + partially patent frontal suture
  • Widened wrists — metaphyseal flaring
  • Pigeon chest (pectus carinatum)
  • Genu varum (bow legs) or genu valgum (knock knees)
  • Waddling gait — proximal myopathy
  • Pot belly — hypotonia
  • Delayed teeth eruption, pitted teeth
🩻 X-ray Findings
Rickets:
  • Cupping, fraying, flaring of metaphysis
  • Widening of epiphyseal plate
  • "String of beads" — rachitic rosary on CXR
  • Osteopenia, cortical thinning
Osteomalacia (Adults):
  • Looser zones (pseudofractures) — PATHOGNOMONIC
  • Bilateral, symmetrical, ⊥ to cortex
  • Sites: femoral neck, pubic rami, scapula, ribs
  • Codfish vertebrae (biconcave)
  • Super scan on bone scan (bilateral symmetric uptake)
⚠️ Tumor-Induced Osteomalacia (TIO) — Key Clinical Clues
  • Middle-aged adult with progressive bone pain + muscle weakness + fragility fractures
  • Labs: Chronic hypophosphatemia + renal P loss, Normal Ca, Normal/mild ↑ PTH, ↑ ALP, Normal 25(OH)D & 1,25(OH)D
  • Elevated FGF23 — key biomarker
  • Small mesenchymal tumor — difficult to locate! Use functional imaging first (octreotide scan, SPECT-CT, 18F-FDG)
  • Treatment: surgical resection of tumor → cure

💊 Osteoporosis

Low bone mass + microarchitectural deterioration → ↑ fracture risk
T-score ≤ -2.5FRAXDXABisphosphonates
🔎 Clinical Approach
1
Assess fracture risk — not just BMD!

Fragility fracture history, clinical risk factors, FRAX (Thailand), fall risk. DXA ≠ sole indicator

2
EXCLUDE metabolic bone diseases first!

Check Ca, PO4, ALP, PTH, 25(OH)D, CBC, LFT, Cr. LOW BMD ≠ OSTEOPOROSIS until excluded

3
Identify secondary causes

Glucocorticoids (most common drug cause), hypogonadism, hyperparathyroidism, hyperthyroidism, malabsorption

4
Categorize fracture risk → guide treatment

Low / Moderate / High / Very High risk → different treatment intensity

🔢 WHO T-score Classification
T-scoreDiagnosis
≥ −1.0Normal
−1.0 to −2.5Osteopenia (Low bone mass)
≤ −2.5Osteoporosis
≤ −2.5 + fragility FxSevere Osteoporosis

⚠️ Use lowest T-score at any site. Applicable to postmenopausal women & men ≥50y only. Use Z-score for younger patients.

💊 Therapeutic Agents
Anti-resorptive:
  • Bisphosphonates: alendronate, risedronate, zoledronic acid
  • Denosumab (RANKL inhibitor)
  • SERMs: raloxifene
  • Menopausal HRT
Osteoanabolic (Very High Risk):
  • Teriparatide (PTH analogue) → then anti-resorptive
  • Romosozumab ("dual action") → then anti-resorptive
⚠️ Imminent Fracture Risk — Very High Risk Criteria (TOPF 2021)
  • Fragility vertebral or hip fracture within 12 months in ≥65y with T-score ≤ −2.5
  • Recurrent vertebral fractures ≥2 levels moderate-severe degree
  • Bilateral hip fractures, or multiple fractures (3 times in 3 sites)
  • Fragility fracture while on osteoporosis drugs ≥2 years
  • T-score ≤ −3.5 in women ≥65y or men ≥70y

🌙 Cushing Syndrome

Chronic excess glucocorticoids — Exogenous (#1) or Endogenous
ACTH-dep vs indepCD 80-90%Screening tests
⏱️ Onset Clues — Rapid vs Slow
  • Cushing's Disease (pituitary): Slow progressive, months-years. Subtle features initially. Most common endogenous cause (80–90% ACTH-dependent)
  • Adrenal adenoma: Gradual, classic cushingoid features, ACTH-independent
  • ACC (adrenal carcinoma): RAPID onset + virilization + weight loss → strongly suspect ACC
  • Ectopic ACTH: Rapid onset + hyperpigmentation + weight loss + severe hypokalemia
  • Carney complex (PPNAD): Cyclic Cushing, young patient, lentigines
🔎 Step-by-Step Approach: Case Detection → Confirmation → Localization
1
Case Detection — Clinical Diagnosis

Highly discriminative: easy bruising, proximal muscle weakness, facial plethora, wide purple striae (>1cm). Screen if adrenal incidentaloma + compatible features

2
Screening Tests (pick 1–2)

1mg overnight DST (cortisol >1.8 mcg/dL positive), 24h urine free cortisol (UFC >3–4× ULN), Late night salivary cortisol (>0.27 mcg/dL), LDDST

3
Exclude Pseudo-Cushing

Morbid obesity, depression, alcoholism, pregnancy, uncontrolled DM. These may have positive screening

4
Localization: ACTH level

ACTH <5 pg/mL → ACTH-independent (adrenal) → adrenal CT. ACTH >10 pg/mL → ACTH-dependent → pituitary MRI + HDDST or IPSS

5
Treatment: Surgery

Transsphenoidal (CD), laparoscopic adrenalectomy (adrenal). Postop: adrenal insufficiency expected → hydrocortisone replacement for 12–36 months

⚠️ High-Yield Discriminating Signs
  • Wide purple striae >1cm — most specific
  • Easy bruising — collagen impairment
  • Proximal muscle weakness — cannot rise from squat
  • Facial plethora
  • Moon face, buffalo hump, central obesity (common but non-specific)
  • HTN, DM, osteoporosis in young patient
  • Decreased growth velocity + weight gain in children
🔬 ACTH-Independent Causes
  • Adrenal adenoma (most common ACTH-indep)
  • Adrenal carcinoma (ACC) — rapid onset + virilization
  • PPNAD (Carney complex) — cyclic, spotty pigmentation, cardiac myxoma, PRKAR1A mutation
  • PBMAH — bilateral macronodular, ARMC5 mutation
🚨 Practical Pearls — Cushing Red Flags
Cushing + weight LOSS
  • Malignancy: ACC
  • Ectopic ACTH
  • CS + infection
Cushing + Virilization
  • ⭐ FAVOR ACC
  • Temporal balding
  • Clitoromegaly
RAPID onset Cushing
  • ACC
  • Malignant ectopic ACTH
  • Small cell lung cancer

🩸 Primary Aldosteronism (PA)

Most common cause of endocrine hypertension — 5–10% of all HTN
HTN + hypokalemiaARR screeningAVS gold std
⏱️ Onset & Presentation Pattern
  • Age: Usually 20–60 years; if <20y → suspect hereditary (GRA)
  • HTN pattern: Often treatment-resistant, multiple antihypertensives needed
  • Hypokalemia: Present in only 28–40% — NOT required for diagnosis
  • Aldosterone escape: Limits edema (ANP → ↑ GFR), so peripheral edema uncommon despite Na retention
  • Insidious: Complications (CV, renal, stroke, DM) exceed expected for HTN severity alone
🔎 5-Step Management Algorithm (Endocrine Society 2016)
1
Case Detection — Who to screen?

Treatment-resistant HTN, HTN + spontaneous/diuretic-induced hypokalemia, HTN + adrenal incidentaloma, HTN + AF, HTN + family history PA/stroke <40y, HTN + OSAS

2
Screening: ARR (Aldosterone:Renin Ratio)

PAC >15 ng/dL + ARR >20(–30) + PRA <1 ng/mL/hr = positive screen. Adjust medications! Stop spironolactone 4 weeks, ACEI/ARB/BB/CCB 2 weeks

3
Confirmation Test

Saline loading test (NaCl 500mL/hr × 4h): PAC >10 ng/dL confirms PA. Or Captopril challenge: PAC decrease <30% confirms PA

4
Subtype Classification: Adrenal CT + AVS

AVS = gold standard for subtyping. Adrenal CT has limitations (micro-APA missed). LI >4:1 = unilateral

5
Treatment

Unilateral APA → laparoscopic adrenalectomy (HTN cured 50%). Bilateral IHA → MRA (spironolactone 12.5–25mg/d). Goal: PRA >1 ng/mL/hr

⚠️ Key Clinical Clues
  • Hypertension + hypokalemia + metabolic alkalosis → think mineralocorticoid excess
  • Serum Na high-normal or above ULN (reset osmostat)
  • Nephrogenic DI, impaired insulin secretion from chronic hypoK
  • Cardiovascular complications DISPROPORTIONATE to HTN severity
  • Atrial fibrillation in young with HTN
  • Spironolactone side effects: gynecomastia, menstrual irregularity

⚡ Pheochromocytoma / Paraganglioma

Catecholamine-secreting tumors — The "Great Mimic" of Medicine
Pheo = adrenal medullaPGL = extra-adrenalRule of 10s
⏱️ Onset — Paroxysmal or Sustained
  • Classic paroxysmal (30%): Episodes of headache + sweating + palpitations lasting minutes to hours, triggered by stress, medications, posture change
  • Sustained HTN: Less dramatic but persistent. 5–15% are normotensive
  • Adrenal crisis-like: Medication-triggered crisis (see table). Hypertensive urgency with antihypertensives
  • Young onset HTN: Any patient <20y with HTN → screen for Pheo
  • Hereditary: 20–30% are hereditary (VHL, NF-1, MEN2A/B, SDHx) → usually younger age
🔎 Clinical Approach — Who to Screen
  • Paroxysmal symptoms: headache + sweating + palpitations (classic triad — 30%)
  • Sustained or paroxysmal HTN (especially <20y)
  • Adrenal incidentaloma with HU >10
  • Hereditary predisposition or syndromic features (VHL, NF-1, MEN2A/B)
  • Idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy
  • Previous history of PPGL
⚠️ Key Clinical Clues (Classic Triad)
  • Headache — paroxysmal, severe
  • Sweating attacks
  • Palpitations / tachycardia
  • Sustained or paroxysmal HTN
  • Pallor (not flushing!)
  • Hyperglycemia — catecholamine effect
  • Orthostatic hypotension — NE depletes blood volume
  • Hypercalcemia — PTHrP or MEN2A
  • Takotsubo cardiomyopathy — rare but classic
🔬 Hereditary Syndromes Associated
  • MEN2A: Pheo + medullary thyroid Ca + PHPT
  • MEN2B: Pheo + MTC + mucosal neuromas + marfanoid
  • VHL: Pheo + clear cell RCC + hemangioblastoma
  • NF-1: Pheo + café-au-lait + neurofibromas
  • SDHx mutations: Paraganglioma + Pheo (extra-adrenal)
  • Total hereditary: 20–30% of all PPGLs
🧪 Biochemical Diagnosis
  • Plasma normetanephrine/metanephrine (highest sensitivity)
  • 24h urine normetanephrine/metanephrine
  • Plasma/urine 3-methoxytyramine (dopamine-secreting)
  • 24h urine VMA (lower sensitivity)
  • Localization: CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast
  • Functional imaging: 131I-MIBG, 18F-FDG PET-CT
  • Preoperative: Alpha-blockade FIRST (phenoxybenzamine), then beta-blockade
  • Never beta-block FIRST → hypertensive crisis!

🔻 Adrenal Insufficiency

Primary (Addison's) vs Secondary (Pituitary) — Life-threatening if missed
Primary AISecondary AIAdrenal crisis
⏱️ Onset Pattern
  • Chronic primary AI (Addison's): Insidious onset over months-years. Fatigue, weight loss, anorexia, salt craving, hyperpigmentation
  • Chronic secondary AI: More subtle, no hyperpigmentation, no hyperkalemia. Often after steroid withdrawal
  • Adrenal crisis: Acute, life-threatening. Triggered by stress (infection, surgery, trauma) in a patient with undiagnosed or undertreated AI. Presents as hypotension + fever + altered consciousness

🔬 Physical Findings to Note
  • Hyperpigmentation — buccal mucosa, skin folds, scars, nipples, pressure points (primary AI only)
  • Alabaster pale skin — secondary AI (ACTH ↓)
  • Loss of axillary/pubic hair — adrenal androgen ↓ (in women)
  • Postural hypotension, dehydration
  • Salt craving (primary AI)
  • Lymphocytosis, eosinophilia on CBC
🚨 Adrenal Crisis — Treat IMMEDIATELY, Don't Wait for Labs
  • Presentation: Hypotension + fever + nausea/vomiting + ↓consciousness + unexplained hypoglycemia
  • Hydrocortisone 100mg IV → then 200mg/24h infusion (or 50–100mg IM q6h)
  • Saline resuscitation 1L NS in first hour
  • Hypoglycemia: D25W 2–4 mL/kg slowly
  • Mineralocorticoid NOT needed if hydrocortisone >50mg/24h
  • Sick day rules: double/triple dose for illness; inject if cannot take orally

🧠 Prolactinoma

Most common pituitary adenoma — Lactotroph adenoma
Peak age 25-34yDopamine agonist firstHook effect
⏱️ Onset & Clinical Presentation by Sex
  • Female (often microprolactinoma): Galactorrhea + oligomenorrhea/amenorrhea + infertility. Hypogonadism symptoms: bone loss, dyspareunia. Usually diagnosed early (symptoms obvious)
  • Male (often macroprolactinoma): Hypogonadism (erectile dysfunction, decreased libido, infertility), mass effects (headache, bitemporal hemianopia). Diagnosed late (symptoms subtle)
  • Peak age: 25–34 years for both sexes
  • Galactorrhea: Occurs in women; uncommon in men
🔎 Approach to Hyperprolactinemia
1
Exclude physiological causes first

Pregnancy, breastfeeding, coitus, exercise, stress, sleep. Always check pregnancy!

2
Review medications

Antipsychotics, metoclopramide, TCA, SSRI, verapamil, cimetidine. Risperidone + metoclopramide can cause PRL >200 µg/L

3
Check TSH (hypothyroidism → ↑TRH → lactotroph stimulation)

Check LFT (liver disease), Cr (CKD → ↓PRL clearance)

4
PRL level correlates with tumor size

Microprolactinoma: PRL >250 µg/L. Macroprolactinoma: PRL >500 µg/L. If macroadenoma ≥3cm but PRL <250 → Hook effect! Dilute 1:100

5
Treatment: Dopamine agonists (first-line)

Cabergoline (preferred): lower side effects, better efficacy. Bromocriptine. Cardiac valvulopathy if cabergoline >2mg/wk cumulative dose

⚠️ Key Clues to Remember
  • Stalk effect: Large non-functioning macroadenoma compresses pituitary stalk → moderate PRL elevation (usually <200 µg/L), not true prolactinoma
  • Hook effect: Very high PRL saturates sandwich assay → falsely LOW result. Dilute sample!
  • Macroprolactinemia: IgG-bound PRL complex, asymptomatic. PEG precipitation: PRL recovery <40% = macroprolactinemia → reassure, no treatment
  • OCP can be used for microadenoma with amenorrhea (if no symptoms requiring treatment)

🦶 Acromegaly

GH-secreting pituitary adenoma → chronic GH hypersecretion
IGF-1 screeningOGTT-GH confirmTSS first-line
⏱️ Onset — Insidious, Often Diagnosed Late
  • Very insidious: Average delay to diagnosis 7–10 years. Patients notice ring no longer fits, shoe size increasing, facial features changing
  • Gigantism: GH excess BEFORE epiphyseal closure → tall stature (early clue in children)
  • Acromegaly: GH excess AFTER epiphyseal closure → acral enlargement without height change
  • Atypical presentation: OSA, DM, HTN, CTS, arthritis, hyperhidrosis in middle-aged adult → screen IGF-1
  • Macroadenoma 77% at diagnosis (late presentation)
⚠️ Classic Physical Findings
  • Enlarged hands & feet — "soft bread dough" feel, widened distal phalanges
  • Prognathism (underbite) + spreading teeth
  • Macroglossia, macrolips, macrognathia
  • Frontal bossing, enlarged supraorbital ridges
  • Skin tags, oily skin, hyperhidrosis
  • Goiter (visceromegaly)
  • Acanthosis nigricans
  • Cutis verticis gyrata — scalp folds
  • Carpal tunnel syndrome
🔬 Radiological Clues
  • Skull lateral: Ballooning of sella, frontal air sinus dilatation, prognathism, widened teeth spacing
  • Both hands AP: Enlarged phalangeal tufts, osteophytes, widened joint spaces, soft tissue thickening, pseudoforamina / spade-like
  • Lateral foot: Heel pad ≥23mm = positive heel pad sign
  • MRI pituitary: Macroadenoma (77%), assess optic chiasm compression
🔎 Diagnosis & Treatment
Diagnosis:
  • Screen: IGF-1 (age-specific) — circulating t½ 15h
  • Confirm: OGTT with GH → nadir GH ≥1 ng/mL = acromegaly
  • Localize: MRI pituitary
  • Screen comorbidities: DM, HTN, OSA, colonoscopy (polyps↑), thyroid U/S
Treatment:
  • Surgery: transsphenoidal (first-line)
  • Goal: normal IGF-1 + random GH <1 ng/mL
  • Assess remission at 12 weeks post-op
  • Medical: octreotide LAR, lanreotide, cabergoline, pegvisomant
  • Radiation: last resort
⚠️ Falsely Low IGF-1 (Don't Miss Acromegaly)
  • Hypothyroidism, glucocorticoids, renal failure (↑BUN), poorly controlled DM, oral estrogen, liver disease, malnutrition, chronic illness, biotin interference

🚨 Pituitary Apoplexy

Rapid hemorrhage or infarction of pituitary tumor — Life-threatening emergency
Thunderclap headacheVisual lossCT first
⏱️ Onset — ACUTE, often dramatic
  • Sudden onset severe headache: "Thunderclap", retroorbital or bifrontal, worst headache of life
  • 20–25% are subclinical (found incidentally on imaging)
  • Demographics: M > F, mean age 50–60 years
  • Background: 45–82% occur in NFPA, 5.5–31% prolactinomas, 7–25% GH-secreting adenomas
  • Trigger: Anticoagulation, surgery, pregnancy, dynamic testing, head trauma
⚠️ Classic Clinical Triad
1️⃣ Thunderclap Headache
  • Sudden, severe
  • Retroorbital > bifrontal
  • Due to dural traction / meningeal irritation
2️⃣ Visual Impairment
  • Bitemporal hemianopia
  • Reduced visual acuity
  • Ophthalmoplegia / ptosis (CN III, IV, VI)
3️⃣ Altered Consciousness
  • ↓ level of consciousness
  • Hypotension (ACTH deficiency)
  • ACTH > Gn > TSH, GH (~90% hypopit)
🔍 Differential Diagnosis
  • Subarachnoid hemorrhage — most important DDx
  • Bacterial meningitis
  • Midbrain infarction
  • Cavernous sinus thrombosis
🚑 Management
  • Imaging first: CT brain NC (emergency) → MRI pituitary if stable
  • Pituitary function: Check cortisol, TSH, Na
  • Corticosteroids: If morning cortisol <19 mcg/dL or hemodynamically unstable → hydrocortisone immediately
  • Surgery indications: Progressive visual/neurological deterioration, impaired consciousness, VF/VA impairment
  • ⚠️ Isolated ophthalmoplegia is NOT a surgical indication
  • Best outcomes <7 days from onset

🤱 Sheehan Syndrome

Postpartum hypopituitarism — Pituitary necrosis after massive PPH
Postpartum hemorrhageFailure to lactateHypopituitarism
⏱️ Onset — Often Delayed Recognition
  • Immediate (acute): Failure to lactate after delivery (first clue!), failure to resume menses
  • Subacute (weeks-months): Fatigue, weakness, anorexia, weight loss, cold intolerance (secondary hypothyroidism)
  • Delayed (years-decades): Progressive hypopituitarism recognized years after delivery. Patient may not connect to past PPH event
  • Adrenal crisis: Can occur with physiological stress (illness, surgery) if AI goes unrecognized
⚠️ Key Clinical Clues
  • History of massive PPH (usually home delivery in developing countries, or emergency C/S)
  • Failure to lactate after delivery — first and most specific symptom
  • Failure to resume menstruation after delivery
  • Axillary/pubic hair loss in women (adrenal androgen deficiency)
  • Empty sella syndrome on MRI (pituitary atrophy after necrosis)
  • Progressive multi-hormone deficiency: GH > Gn > TSH > ACTH
🔬 Imaging & Management
  • MRI: Empty sella (pituitary atrophy)
  • Hormone levels: all anterior pituitary hormones potentially low
  • Stimulation tests: confirm individual deficiencies
  • Lifelong hormone replacement (hydrocortisone, levothyroxine, sex hormones)
  • DDAVP if central DI develops
  • Sick day rules education essential
  • Patient education on adrenal crisis prevention

🌸 Amenorrhea

Primary vs Secondary — Systematic Anatomical Approach
Exclude pregnancy firstFSH/LHOutflow tract → Ovary → Pituitary → Hypothalamus
⏱️ Onset Definitions
⏱️ Primary Amenorrhea
  • Never menstruated + no 2° sex characteristics by age 14 (13)y
  • Never menstruated despite normal growth + 2° sex characteristics by age 16 (15)y
  • Or 2 years after breast development without menses
⏱️ Secondary Amenorrhea
  • Absence ≥6 months (irregular cycle)
  • Absence ≥3× previous cycle intervals (regular cycle)
  • ⚠️ ALWAYS exclude pregnancy first!
🔎 Primary Amenorrhea — Decision Tree
WITH Breast Development:
  • Check uterus (US/exam)
  • Normal uterus + outflow obstruction → imperforate hymen, vaginal septum, cervical atresia
  • Normal uterus + no obstruction → CDGP or work up as secondary amenorrhea
  • No uterus + 46XX → Müllerian agenesis (MRKH)
  • No uterus + 46XY + palpable gonad → CAIS (Complete Androgen Insensitivity) → gonadectomy risk
WITHOUT Breast Development:
  • Check uterus
  • Uterus present → hypogonadism → check FSH/LH
  • High FSH → Hypergonadotropic → Turner, gonadal dysgenesis, CYP17 deficiency
  • Low FSH → Hypogonadotropic → MRI pituitary → Kallmann (tall stature, anosmia), hypothyroid, Cushing
  • No uterus → 46XY DSD → CYP17 def, Leydig cell hypoplasia
🔎 Secondary Amenorrhea — Approach
1
Exclude pregnancy (ALWAYS first)

urine/serum hCG

2
Check FSH/LH + Prolactin + TSH

FSH elevated → POI (premature ovarian insufficiency) or Turner mosaic. FSH low/normal → check prolactin, thyroid, exclude eating disorder

3
Signs of androgen excess?

If yes → check testosterone, DHEAS, 17-OHP, exclude ovarian/adrenal tumor, consider PCOS or non-classic CAH

4
Functional hypothalamic amenorrhea (FHA)

Exclude organic causes. Eating disorders, excessive exercise, weight loss, chronic illness. Low FSH/LH, low E2, normal prolactin

⚠️ Examination Findings in Amenorrhea — Systematic
  • Short stature + low hairline: Turner syndrome
  • Tall stature + anosmia: Kallmann syndrome
  • Groin mass (gonad): CAIS or Müllerian agenesis
  • Virilization + short stature: CAH, Cushing
  • No axillary/pubic hair + breast development + XY: CAIS (androgen insensitivity)
  • Galactorrhea: Prolactinoma, drugs
  • Acne + hirsutism: PCOS, CAH, androgen-secreting tumor
  • Ambiguous genitalia at birth: CAH (21-OH deficiency) in females

🔄 PCOS — Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome

Most common endocrine disorder in women — Lifelong metabolic syndrome
Rotterdam 20182/3 criteriaInsulin resistance
⏱️ Onset — Puberty-onset, Slowly Progressive
  • Puberty: Acne + irregular periods + hirsutism from teenage years → slowly progressive course
  • Adulthood: Obesity emergence (>50% of PCOS patients), worsening hyperandrogenemia with increasing adiposity
  • Most common endocrine disorder in women of reproductive age
  • NOT acute onset — gradual worsening. Rapid virilization → think tumor (testosterone >200 ng/dL or DHEAS >700 µg/dL)
⚠️ Rotterdam 2018 Diagnostic Criteria (2/3)
  • 1. Oligomenorrhea / chronic anovulation (≤8 cycles/year or cycle >35 days)
  • 2. Clinical AND/OR biochemical hyperandrogenism (hirsutism mFG ≥4–6, acne, alopecia; OR elevated testosterone/free testosterone)
  • 3. Polycystic ovarian morphology (PCOM): ≥20 follicles 2–9mm per ovary OR volume >10mL
  • Must EXCLUDE other causes: non-classic CAH (17-OHP), thyroid disease, hyperprolactinemia, Cushing
🔬 Physical Findings
  • Hirsutism — mFG score ≥3 (Thailand) or ≥4–6 (PCOS guideline 2023)
  • Acne — nodular cystic pattern (severe = check androgens)
  • Acanthosis nigricans — insulin resistance marker
  • Obesity — especially central (waist circumference)
  • Normal or mildly virilized — if frank virilization → rule out tumor
  • Ovarian cysts on US — "string of pearls" appearance
💊 Treatment Approach
For Hirsutism/Acne:
  • OCP (first-line anti-androgen)
  • Spironolactone
  • Cyproterone acetate
  • Finasteride
  • Laser hair removal
For Menstrual Irregularity:
  • Cyclic progestins
  • OCP (24+4 regimen)
  • Metformin (if metabolic risk)
  • Weight loss (if obese)
For Fertility:
  • Delay anti-androgen drugs
  • Letrozole (preferred)
  • Clomiphene
  • Gonadotropins
  • IVF if needed

🔬 Hyperandrogenism

Excess androgen effects — From hirsutism to frank virilization
mFG scoreT >200 = tumorVirilization = serious
⏱️ Onset Clues — Gradual vs Rapid
  • Gradual (months-years): PCOS, non-classic CAH, familial hirsutism — most common
  • Rapid onset virilization (<6 months): TUMOR until proven otherwise — adrenocortical carcinoma, ovarian tumor (Sertoli-Leydig), adrenal tumor
  • Fetal life: Ambiguous genitalia (CAH in 46,XX)
  • Childhood: Precocious pubarche, accelerated growth, advanced BA
  • Adulthood: Primary/secondary amenorrhea, menstrual irregularity, anovulation, infertility
⚠️ Virilization Signs — Severe Androgen Excess
  • Temporal balding (male-pattern alopecia)
  • Deepening of voice
  • Breast atrophy
  • Increased muscle mass
  • Clitoromegaly: length >1cm, clitoral index >35mm²
  • Acne + hirsutism together
  • ⭐ If ANY virilization sign → RULE OUT TUMOR first!
🔬 Androgen Levels — Interpretation
  • Total testosterone <200 ng/dL: Hyperandrogenic chronic anovulation (PCOS, non-classic CAH)
  • Total testosterone >200 ng/dL: Androgen-secreting tumor (ACC, ovarian)
  • DHEAS <700 µg/dL: PCOS or CAH (adrenal source)
  • DHEAS >700 µg/dL: Adrenal tumor
  • 17-OHP (8AM) elevated: Consider CAH (21-OH deficiency) — confirm with ACTH stimulation test
📏 Modified Ferriman-Gallwey (mFG) Score

Score areas: upper lip, chin, chest, upper abdomen, lower abdomen, thighs, upper arms, upper back, lower back

  • mFG ≥ 3 = hirsutism (Thailand)
  • mFG ≥ 4–6 = hirsutism (PCOS guideline 2023)
Hirsutism vs Hypertrichosis:
  • Hirsutism: Excess terminal hair in ANDROGEN-DEPENDENT areas (androgen excess)
  • Hypertrichosis: Generalized vellus hair, NON-sexual areas, NOT androgen-dependent (medications: phenytoin, cyclosporine, minoxidil; hypothyroidism, anorexia)

👤 Gynecomastia

Benign proliferation of glandular breast tissue in males — Relative estrogen excess
Estrogen↑ or Androgen↓Rule out cancer3 peaks of life
⏱️ Onset — Three Physiologic Peaks
  • 1. Neonatal: Maternal estrogen transfer → resolves in weeks
  • 2. Pubertal (50% of mid-pubertal boys, age 13–14y): ↑ estradiol precedes ↑ testosterone. 90% resolve spontaneously within 1–2 years. DDx: medications, hereditary
  • 3. Adult/Elderly (>50y, 65% of men 50–80y): ↓ testosterone, ↑ adiposity, ↑ SHBG → ↓ free testosterone
  • Rapid onset or unilateral hard mass → rule out BREAST CANCER
🔎 Clinical Approach
1
Confirm it's gynecomastia, not pseudogynecomastia (lipomastia)

True gynecomastia: palpable, concentric, subareolar glandular tissue disk, typically tender. Lipomastia: soft adipose, no glandular tissue

2
Exclude breast cancer

Unilateral, hard, eccentric, non-tender, nipple discharge, skin changes, lymphadenopathy → biopsy!

3
Assess for physiologic cause

Pubertal, neonatal, elderly? Drug review? Recent weight gain?

4
Labs: testosterone, LH, FSH, E2, hCG, DHEAS, TSH, LFT, Cr

hCG elevation → testicular germ cell tumor or ectopic hCG. ↑LH/FSH + ↓T → primary hypogonadism (Klinefelter). Low LH/FSH + low T → secondary hypogonadism

⚠️ Pathological Causes to Know
  • Klinefelter (47,XXY): Small firm testes, ↑FSH/LH, ↓T → most common pathological cause
  • Testicular tumors: Germ cell (hCG), Sertoli cell, Leydig cell → ↑E2
  • Liver cirrhosis / alcohol: ↑E2 (↑aromatase), ↓testosterone
  • Hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism
  • CKD: ↓testosterone clearance
  • Refeeding syndrome
  • Androgen ablation therapy for prostate cancer
🔬 Drug-Induced — High Yield List
  • Spironolactone (MRA — most common drug cause)
  • Ketoconazole, cimetidine, digoxin
  • Anabolic steroids (bodybuilders)
  • Cannabis
  • Estrogens (topical/oral)
  • Antiandrogens (bicalutamide, flutamide)
  • GnRH agonists (leuprolide for prostate Ca)
  • Metoclopramide, antipsychotics (via hyperprolactinemia)
